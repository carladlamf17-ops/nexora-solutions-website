The email impersonating your bank

The display name, sender address, and button destination are separate clues. Checking them independently exposes messages that copy a bank’s visual design.

Nexora SolutionsIssue 01November 9, 2026

The subject line says your account will lock in half an hour. In the example, the button promises to restore access, but hovering reveals a different domain. That pause before clicking changes how the whole email reads.

The FTC documents emails that invent account problems or invoices to get people to open links. Gmail explains how to inspect domain authentication. A message authenticated for an attacker-controlled domain can still be phishing. See sources

Simulated example

Read the text in the image
  • From: Banco Ejemplo <avisos@acceso.example>
  • Reply-To: soporte@tramite.example
  • Button: Restore access
  • Hover destination: https://banco.acceso.example/validar

“Your account will be locked” Simulated scenes

Look at what the attacker controls

They can copy the bank name and logo, put banco in a subdomain, and direct replies elsewhere. The example server is banco.acceso.example, under acceso.example.

Use an independent reference

Open your banking app or bookmark. If you do not see the notice there, contact the bank through a known channel. A padlock only indicates an encrypted connection.

Practical guide

What to do, step by step

  1. Expand the sender and compare the full domain with one you already know.
  2. On desktop, hover over the button without opening it. On mobile, use the official app if you cannot read the full address.
  3. Report phishing. If you gave away a password, change it in the official service and sign out other sessions.
