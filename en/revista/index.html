Phishing under the microscope: check before you click

The message can have your name, the right logo and perfect spelling. Before acting, ask two separate questions: who sent it, and where does the link go?

Inspect a link without opening it

  1. Look at the actual destination. On a desktop, hover over the button and read the address shown by the browser or email client. If you cannot see it in full on mobile, leave it unopened and look for the request in the official app.
  2. Find the server in the link. Start after https:// and stop at the first slash, question mark or hash sign. If that part contains an at sign, the server follows it. In the examples below you expect “banco.example”. Compare the full address with one you already bookmarked. Avoid assuming the last two labels always identify the owner. Some public suffixes have multiple labels, such as .com.ve.
  3. Recognize what can stay hidden. HTTPS and the padlock protect the connection, but a fake site can use them too. A short link or QR code can hide the destination. Even a visible address can redirect. If the message asks for money, passwords or codes, open the service yourself.

The bank in this exercise uses banco.example. Identify the server in each case, then expand the breakdown. The fictional URLs are shown as plain text.

Take apart a suspicious email

Fictional example

From: Banco Ejemplo <avisos@acceso.example>
Reply-To: soporte@tramite.example
Subject: Your account will be locked in 30 minutes
Visible button: “Restore access”

  1. Expand the sender details. The display name says “Banco Ejemplo” but the address is at acceso.example. If your client shows a Reply-To address, check whether it points somewhere unexpected. Avoid replying to verify it.
  2. Look at what they ask you to do. A 30-minute deadline and a sign-in button are meant to get you to act before checking. If they ask for an SMS code or send an unexpected attachment such as an invoice, verify through a known channel first. A filename such as invoice.pdf.exe ends in .exe.
  3. Use Gmail’s clues. Open the details next to the sender and check “mailed by” and “signed by” if shown. A message can be authenticated for a domain controlled by the attacker. If something looks wrong, use the report phishing option. At work, notify IT through your internal channel.

If you fell for it, act based on what you did

  1. You only opened the link. Close the page. If it downloaded a file, leave it unopened. If you opened a suspicious file or installed something, notify IT or run an up-to-date security scan.
  2. You entered a password or code. Use the official app or website on a trusted device. Change the password, sign out other sessions and review the recovery email or phone number. Change that password on any other service where you reused it.
  3. You shared banking details or authorized a payment. Call your bank immediately through its official channel. Ask it to review your transactions and save the message, time and references.

01

“Cousin, I got a new number”: the WhatsApp dollar trap

“Cousin, I changed my number. I’m selling dollars cheap. Send me a Pago Móvil payment now and I’ll set them aside.” The message may come from a new number using a relative’s photo or from their actual WhatsApp account if someone took it over. An unusually good rate, pressure to pay immediately, and bank details belonging to someone else are warning signs.

The payment goes to a different person

Fictional example. After claiming to have a new number, the supposed relative asks you to pay into someone else’s account. If they call through the chat or send a voice note, place your own call to the number you already saved. If no one answers, wait.

How to protect yourself

Call the number you already saved using a regular phone call outside WhatsApp, or confirm in person. If you cannot verify the offer, hold off on payment. If you confirm impersonation, warn your relative and other contacts through another channel, save the chat, and report the contact in WhatsApp. If you already transferred money, contact your bank immediately through an official channel and keep the reference number.

  • CICPC warning about fake dollar sales · Venevisión (Spanish)

02

“I already paid!”: the fake receipt that can cost you the order

A supposed buyer sends a screenshot over WhatsApp and pressures you to hand over the order. The image may have been edited or may show a different transaction. Watch for an incorrect amount or date, a reused reference number, or mismatched recipient details. Even a convincing screenshot can be misleading.

Two orders can cost exactly the same

Fictional example. Two orders cost Bs. 1,250. A buyer forwards the receipt for the first one to collect the second. Check the receiving account and match each credit to just one order. Compare the reference if your bank shows it. If the app is down or the transaction is unclear, keep the order pending and check with your bank.

How to protect yourself

Open your bank’s official app or website yourself. Look for the credit in the account linked to Pago Móvil and check the amount, date, and reference if available. If it does not appear, wait for confirmation before handing over goods or refunding a supposed overpayment. If you already handed over the order, keep the chat, screenshot, and order details. Check with your bank and take the evidence to the CICPC.

  • How to check Pago Móvil transactions · Banesco (Spanish)

03

Your balance goes up, then they charge you to withdraw

A Ponzi scheme pays earlier participants with money from newer ones. Payments dry up when new funds stop coming in. A task scam promises money for watching ads or clicking, shows a balance on screen, then asks you to top up to continue or withdraw. A small initial payout can be bait.

One top-up leads to another

Fictional example. After a small withdrawal, the platform asks for a fee to unlock your balance. It then demands another top-up to fix a supposed error. Save the terms, chats, and receipts and stop sending money.

How to protect yourself

Watch for fixed returns without a verifiable business, pressure to recruit, and fees to unlock a balance. Check who operates the offer, where the payouts come from, and the withdrawal terms before sending money. Stop if asked to deposit money to get paid for a task.

  • How Ponzi schemes work · Investor.gov
  • Task scams · FTC (Spanish)

04

AdSelect and offers to watch ads

Pages using the AdSelect name advertise rewards for advertising activities. A page presenting itself as AdSelect also warns of supposed administrators promising returns or charging to unlock balances. This is the platform’s own statement. We found no ruling from an authority classifying AdSelect as a Ponzi scheme.

How to protect yourself

If someone approaches you using that name, independently verify the domain and who contacted you. If asked for a top-up, cryptocurrency, or a fee to withdraw, stop responding and save screenshots. A familiar name or payment receipt shared in a group cannot verify the offer.

05

“We’ll cut off your line”: the fake technician after your code

Fictional examples include “We’re upgrading your plan and need your code” and “Send a Pago Móvil payment in bolívares today or we’ll cut off your line.” Movistar Venezuela documented calls using a supposed 5G upgrade to obtain WhatsApp codes. The same tactic can use any carrier’s name.

The SMS tells you what the code is for

If the message mentions registering WhatsApp or accessing your account, read it without sharing the code. If you already shared it and lost access, follow the WhatsApp recovery steps at the end of this issue.

How to protect yourself

Hang up and check your balance, bill, or plan through your carrier’s official app or website. Keep SMS codes private and avoid QR codes sent to activate your plan. If you owe money, pay through the channel you independently confirmed.

  • Phone fraud warning · Movistar Venezuela (Spanish PDF)

06

“There’s a complaint against you”: the fake summons demanding money

Someone claiming to be from Venezuela’s CICPC investigative police or another agency says a complaint has been filed against you. They may send a supposed summons over WhatsApp with seals and signatures, then demand money or information to stop proceedings. The CICPC director warned that fake summons forms were being used for extortion.

Watch the CICPC news clip

How to protect yourself

End the conversation. Save the message and verify the summons with the relevant office using independently obtained contact details or by visiting in person. Avoid the phone numbers and links on the suspicious notice and decline requests to transfer money to cancel proceedings.

  • CICPC director’s warning reported by La Prensa de Monagas (Spanish)

Investment traps in Venezuela: named cases and a recent warning

On October 30, 2026, Venevisión reported that Venezuela’s securities regulator, Sunaval, warned about misleading investment offers promoted on WhatsApp, Telegram and other digital channels. The published warning describes the tactics without naming specific platforms. Read the warning

Cases previously reported in Venezuela

  • SolesBot · 2024. Unión Radio reported that operations stopped after fraud complaints. A specialist interviewed by the station described the model as a Ponzi scheme. Unión Radio
  • Hyper AI · 2024. Venezuelan users reported problems and a lack of transparency in what BeInCrypto covered as a suspected Ponzi scheme. BeInCrypto
  • HV IJEX · 2025. El Estímulo reported the collapse of the platform after it drew users in Venezuela. El Estímulo

These reports concern past cases. We found no confirmation that those three platforms were still recruiting in Venezuela in November 2026.

If you already paid or shared information

  1. If you sent money

    Contact your bank or payment provider immediately through an official channel. Report the transaction and ask what action they can take. Save receipts, the recipient details, and the date. Avoid paying anyone who promises to recover the money.

  2. If you gave out a WhatsApp code

    Follow the official instructions to re-register your number. Warn your contacts through another channel. Once you regain access, review linked devices and enable two-step verification.

  3. If someone threatened you or impersonated an official

    Save chats, numbers, screenshots, and documents you received. Take the evidence to a CICPC office to report the incident. Keep the originals and avoid exposing personal details when warning others.
