Digital scams in Venezuela

An offer to sell dollars over WhatsApp, a fake receipt, or a supposed police summons can all reach the same phone. Learn the warning signs and what to do if you already responded.

01

A fake relative selling dollars on WhatsApp

“Cousin, I changed my number. I’m selling dollars cheap. Send me a Pago Móvil payment now and I’ll set them aside.” The message may come from a new number using a relative’s photo or from their actual WhatsApp account if someone took it over. An unusually good rate, pressure to pay immediately, and bank details belonging to someone else are warning signs.

How to protect yourself

Call the number you already saved using a regular phone call outside WhatsApp, or confirm in person. If you cannot verify the offer, hold off on payment. If you confirm impersonation, warn your relative and other contacts through another channel, save the chat, and report the contact in WhatsApp. If you already transferred money, contact your bank immediately through an official channel and keep the reference number.

  • CICPC warning about fake dollar sales · Venevisión (Spanish)

02

Fake Pago Móvil payment receipts

A supposed buyer sends a screenshot over WhatsApp and pressures you to hand over the order. The image may have been edited or may show a different transaction. Watch for an incorrect amount or date, a reused reference number, or mismatched recipient details. Even a convincing screenshot can be misleading.

How to protect yourself

Open your bank’s official app or website yourself. Look for the credit in the account linked to Pago Móvil and check the amount, date, and reference if available. If it does not appear, wait for confirmation before handing over goods or refunding a supposed overpayment. If you already handed over the order, keep the chat, screenshot, and order details. Check with your bank and take the evidence to the CICPC.

  • How to check Pago Móvil transactions · Banesco (Spanish)

03

Ponzi schemes and task scams

A Ponzi scheme pays earlier participants with money from newer ones. Payments dry up when new funds stop coming in. A task scam promises money for watching ads or clicking, shows a balance on screen, then asks you to top up to continue or withdraw. A small initial payout can be bait.

How to protect yourself

Watch for fixed returns without a verifiable business, pressure to recruit, and fees to unlock a balance. Check who operates the offer, where the payouts come from, and the withdrawal terms before sending money. Stop if asked to deposit money to get paid for a task.

  • How Ponzi schemes work · Investor.gov
  • Task scams · FTC (Spanish)

04

AdSelect and offers to watch ads

Pages using the AdSelect name advertise rewards for advertising activities. A page presenting itself as AdSelect also warns of supposed administrators promising returns or charging to unlock balances. This is the platform’s own statement. We found no ruling from an authority classifying AdSelect as a Ponzi scheme.

How to protect yourself

If someone approaches you using that name, independently verify the domain and who contacted you. If asked for a top-up, cryptocurrency, or a fee to withdraw, stop responding and save screenshots. A familiar name or payment receipt shared in a group cannot verify the offer.

05

Fake mobile carrier technicians

Fictional examples include “We’re upgrading your plan and need your code” and “Send a Pago Móvil payment in bolívares today or we’ll cut off your line.” Movistar Venezuela documented calls using a supposed 5G upgrade to obtain WhatsApp codes. The same tactic can use any carrier’s name.

How to protect yourself

Hang up and check your balance, bill, or plan through your carrier’s official app or website. Keep SMS codes private and avoid QR codes sent to activate your plan. If you owe money, pay through the channel you independently confirmed.

  • Phone fraud warning · Movistar Venezuela (Spanish PDF)

06

Impersonated officials and fake summonses

Someone claiming to be from Venezuela’s CICPC investigative police or another agency says a complaint has been filed against you. They may send a supposed summons over WhatsApp with seals and signatures, then demand money or information to stop proceedings. The CICPC director warned that fake summons forms were being used for extortion.

How to protect yourself

End the conversation. Save the message and verify the summons with the relevant office using independently obtained contact details or by visiting in person. Avoid the phone numbers and links on the suspicious notice and decline requests to transfer money to cancel proceedings.

  • CICPC director’s warning reported by La Prensa de Monagas (Spanish)

If you already paid or shared information

  1. If you sent money

    Contact your bank or payment provider immediately through an official channel. Report the transaction and ask what action they can take. Save receipts, the recipient details, and the date. Avoid paying anyone who promises to recover the money.

  2. If you gave out a WhatsApp code

    Follow the official instructions to re-register your number. Warn your contacts through another channel. Once you regain access, review linked devices and enable two-step verification.

  3. If someone threatened you or impersonated an official

    Save chats, numbers, screenshots, and documents you received. Take the evidence to a CICPC office to report the incident. Keep the originals and avoid exposing personal details when warning others.
